# Portland vs Geopolymer + Monolithic Catenary Stress-Skin Vaults
**UNE:** DV.CON.FUND.BINDER.CAT.001  
**Status:** draft  
**η note:** Two binder systems + one of the highest-leverage pure-compression shelter forms (Shambhala-style stress-skin / double-catenary). Cellular concrete fills the void.

## One-sentence essence
Compare ordinary Portland cement hydration with geopolymer chemistry, then apply the winner (or hybrid) to monolithic double-catenary stress-skin shells that work almost entirely in compression and can be filled with open-cell cellular concrete.

## 1. Portland Cement vs Geopolymer

| Property                    | Portland Cement (OPC)                  | Geopolymer (alkali-activated)              |
|-----------------------------|----------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------|
| Primary reaction            | Hydration of C₃S / C₂S → C-S-H        | Dissolution + polycondensation of aluminosilicates |
| Main binder phase           | Calcium-silicate-hydrate (C-S-H)       | N-A-S-H or C-A-S-H gel                     |
| CO₂ footprint               | High (calcination of limestone)        | Lower (can use fly ash, slag, calcined clay) |
| Heat of hydration           | High early heat (especially C₃A/C₃S)   | Generally lower and more controllable      |
| Long-term durability        | Excellent in normal exposure           | Often superior in acid / sulfate / fire    |
| Setting control             | Gypsum + temperature                   | Activator concentration + temperature      |
| Field practicality          | Universal, cheap, known                | Requires careful activator handling        |
| Fit with open-cell AeroCement | Excellent base                       | Promising for lower heat & higher durability |

### Typical Heat of Hydration (approximate)

| Cement Type          | 3-day heat (J/g) | 7-day heat (J/g) | 28-day heat (J/g) |
|----------------------|------------------|------------------|-------------------|
| Ordinary Portland    | 250–300          | 300–350          | 350–400           |
| Moderate heat OPC    | 200–250          | 250–300          | 300–350           |
| Geopolymer (fly-ash) | 150–250          | 200–300          | 250–350           |

(Actual values vary strongly with composition, w/b ratio, and temperature.)

## 2. Monolithic Double-Catenary / Stress-Skin Vault (Shambhala-style)

Core idea from Shambhala Village Man / shambhalavillage.us:
- Thin ferrocement or high-strength shell formed as a catenary or parabolic curve (or double-curved variant).
- The shape puts the material almost purely into compression.
- Stress-skin / structural-sandwich construction: two thin strong skins with a lightweight core (cellular concrete, recycled fill, or insulation).
- Monolithic continuity from roof shell down into the footing so the entire structure acts as one compression vessel.
- The footing is flared / belled and hooks into the ground, giving high resistance to uplift and overturning without relying on tensile steel.

### Why pure compression is powerful
- Concrete (and GFRC) is excellent in compression and weak in tension.
- Rebar in ordinary beams is mostly “insurance” after the concrete has already cracked.
- A correctly shaped catenary / vault never needs to develop significant tension under design loads → the material stays in its strong regime for centuries.
- Resulting structures can be extremely durable (centuries to millennia if detailing and drainage are correct) and highly resistant to blast, wind, and seismic forces when properly engineered.

### Filling the void
- The space between the outer stress-skin and any inner surface (or the large internal volume) is filled with cellular / open-cell aerated concrete.
- This adds thermal mass, insulation, and further stiffening while keeping weight low.
- Open-cell character remains available for vapor movement or future desiccant / labyrinth integration.

## 3. Integration with Food & Water Stack
- Large ferrocement tanks (10 000–50 000+ gallon) can be built with the same shell techniques and share formwork knowledge.
- Roof catchment from the catenary surfaces feeds the tanks directly.
- Tanks support tilapia + duckweed (or other aquaponics) loops that close the nutrient and water cycles for the community node.
- One structural language (thin-shell compression + cellular fill) serves shelter, water storage, and food production.
