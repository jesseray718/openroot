Case Study 006: Fractional Reserve Banking, War, and the Money Changers
RNS Classification: Suppressed Science / Institutional Harm
Primary Sources: Murray Rothbard, The Mystery of Banking (1983); G. Edward Griffin, The Creature from Jekyll Island (1994); Stephen Zarlenga, The Lost Science of Money (2002); Smedley Butler, War is a Racket (1935)
Status: Detailed Overview
Summary
In 1913, the United States Congress passed the Federal Reserve Act, transferring the power to create money from the public treasury to a consortium of private banks operating under a veneer of government oversight. The Federal Reserve System was not designed to stabilize the economy. It was designed to concentrate the power of money creation in private hands, enabling a cycle of debt-dependent growth, war financing, and economic extraction that has persisted for over a century. Under fractional reserve banking, banks lend out multiples of what they hold in deposits — effectively creating money from nothing and collecting interest on it. This mechanism transforms money from a public utility into a private commodity, and it fundamentally alters the relationship between labor and value. When wars are fought, they are financed through debt issued by the same banking institutions that profit from both the borrowing and the reconstruction. The pattern is not conspiracy. It is incentive structure. Private banks profit from government debt. Governments go to war. Debt expands. Interest flows upward. The public pays. This case study traces the mechanics of that system from the founding of the Federal Reserve through the debt economies of the 20th and 21st centuries, and connects the pattern to the Renaissance Protocol's alternative: a Proof-of-Build economy where value originates from real work, not from lending created money at interest.

Part 1: How Money Creation Works — And Who Does It
The Mechanics of Fractional Reserve Banking
Under a fractional reserve system, a bank is required to keep only a fraction of its deposits on hand. The rest can be lent out. When a bank issues a loan, it does not lend out existing money from a vault. It creates a new deposit in the borrower's account — effectively creating new money. The borrower spends that money into the economy. The bank collects interest on money it created from an accounting entry.

This is not a fringe claim. The Bank of England published a paper in 2014 titled "Money Creation in the Modern Economy" confirming that commercial banks create money through lending: "Whenever a bank makes a loan, it simultaneously creates a matching deposit in the borrower's bank account, thereby creating new money."

The implications are significant:

Money is created as debt. Every dollar in circulation was created by a loan somewhere. If all debts were repaid, there would be no money in circulation — except the physical currency issued by the government, which constitutes approximately 3% of the US money supply.
Interest is owed on money that did not exist before the loan. The principal is created by the loan. The interest is not. This means the system requires constant expansion of debt to service existing debt. Without new borrowing, defaults are mathematically inevitable.
Banks collect income from money they did not possess. The lender contributes no prior labor or goods. The risk of default exists, but the money itself is conjured from the legal privilege of banking.
The Historical Precedent
This mechanism is not new. Goldsmiths in 17th-century England issued more paper receipts for gold than they held in vaults, effectively creating money through fractional issuance. When too many depositors demanded their gold simultaneously — a bank run — the scheme collapsed. The pattern repeated across centuries: private entities create money, profit from it, shift losses to the public during crises, and lobby for legal protections that preserve the privilege.

Part 2: The Federal Reserve — Private Power, Public Face
The Meeting at Jekyll Island
In November 1910, a group of men representing the wealthiest financial interests in the United States met privately at the Jekyll Island Club off the coast of Georgia. The attendees included:

Nelson Aldrich, Republican Senator and chair of the National Monetary Commission, father-in-law of John D. Rockefeller Jr.
Henry P. Davison, senior partner at J.P. Morgan
Frank Vanderlip, president of National City Bank (now Citibank)
A. Piatt Andrew, Assistant Secretary of the Treasury
Benjamin Strong, head of J.P. Morgan's Bankers Trust, later first head of the New York Federal Reserve
Paul Warburg, representative of Kuhn, Loeb & Co., brother of Max Warburg who directed the Warburg Bank in Germany
The meeting was secret. Attendees used first names only to avoid identification. The purpose was to draft legislation that would create a central banking system for the United States — one that would appear to be a government agency but would operate primarily in the interest of the private banks it served.

What emerged from Jekyll Island became the foundation for the Federal Reserve Act of 1913.

Critical Note on Sources
The Jekyll Island meeting is historically documented. However, the extent to which it determined the final form of the Federal Reserve Act is debated. The Aldrich Plan that came out of Jekyll Island was initially rejected by Congress. It took three more years of political negotiation before the Federal Reserve Act passed under Woodrow Wilson in 1913. The final bill differed from the Jekyll Island plan in important ways — it included more government oversight and a decentralized structure with 12 regional banks.

What is not disputed: the Federal Reserve System was shaped substantially by private banking interests, and its structure preserves significant private bank influence. The Federal Reserve Banks are privately incorporated institutions. Their boards include private bank executives. The Federal Reserve earns interest on the securities it holds and returns surplus to the Treasury — but the mechanism of money creation and the rate-setting process are deeply intertwined with private banking interests.

Woodrow Wilson's Regret
In 1919, six years after signing the Federal Reserve Act, President Woodrow Wilson wrote: "Some of the biggest men in the United States, in the field of commerce and manufacture, are afraid of somebody, are afraid of something. They know that there is a power somewhere so organized, so subtle, so watchful, so interlocked, so complete, so pervasive, that they had better not speak above their breath when they speak in condemnation of it."

Whether Wilson was referring specifically to the Federal Reserve or to broader financial power is debated by historians. The quote is sometimes misattributed or excerpted in misleading ways. What is clear is that Wilson expressed significant concern about concentrated financial power during and after his presidency.

The Structure of the Fed
Key structural features:

The Federal Reserve Board of Governors is a government agency appointed by the President. Seven members, 14-year terms.
The 12 Regional Federal Reserve Banks are privately incorporated. Each has a board of directors that includes private bankers.
The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) sets monetary policy. It includes the 7 governors plus 5 of the 12 regional bank presidents. Regional bank presidents are selected with substantial input from private banks.
Member banks are required to purchase stock in their regional Federal Reserve Bank. This stock pays a fixed 6% dividend. While the Fed returns surplus to the Treasury, the private member banks receive this guaranteed dividend on their ownership stake.
The result is a hybrid structure where the language of public accountability overlays a system that serves private banking interests. When the Fed creates money through quantitative easing, it purchases securities from private banks — increasing bank reserves and enabling further lending. The profits of money creation flow upward. The costs — inflation, asset bubbles, debt burdens — distribute downward.

Part 3: War Financing and the Debt Cycle
How Wars Are Funded
Wars are expensive. A government has three options to fund war spending:

Taxation. Raise taxes to pay for the war directly. This is politically unpopular and transparent — citizens see the cost immediately.

Borrowing. Issue government bonds (debt) sold to banks, institutions, and foreign governments. The government receives money now, owes principal plus interest later. The bonds are purchased with money often created by the banking system itself.

Money creation. The central bank creates new money to purchase government debt directly. This is monetization — effectively printing money. It causes inflation, which acts as a hidden tax on anyone holding currency.

The United States has historically relied heavily on option 2 — borrowing. Every major American war has been financed substantially through debt:

World War I: The Liberty Bond program. Banks purchased bonds and then marketed them to the public. The Federal Reserve, newly created in 1913, supported the bond market by keeping interest rates low. War debt expanded dramatically. J.P. Morgan and Company served as the purchasing agent for Allied war supplies in the United States, earning substantial commissions on billions of dollars of purchases.
World War II: War bonds again. The Federal Reserve pegged interest rates at 0.375% on short-term Treasury securities to keep government borrowing costs low. Government debt rose from approximately 40% of GDP in 1940 to over 110% by 1945.
Vietnam War: Funded through borrowing without tax increases, contributing to the inflation of the late 1960s and 1970s. President Johnson chose not to raise taxes to fund the war, relying instead on deficit spending facilitated by Federal Reserve accommodation.
Post-9/11 Wars: The wars in Afghanistan and Iraq were funded almost entirely through supplemental appropriations — borrowing outside the normal budget process. The Costs of War Project at Brown University estimates total costs exceeding $8 trillion when including veterans' care and interest on war debt.
Who Profits
When the government borrows to finance a war, the lenders — banks and financial institutions — receive interest payments funded by taxpayers. When the central bank facilitates this borrowing by keeping rates low or purchasing bonds, it enables the expansion of debt that generates ongoing interest income for the financial sector.

During wartime, specific banks handle government bond offerings. Specific corporations receive defense contracts. Specific industries expand. The financial benefit concentrates among those already positioned within the banking-defense nexus. The cost — death, injury, trauma, debt, inflation — distributes among the general population.

Smedley Butler, the most decorated Marine in US history at the time of his death, described this directly in War is a Racket (1935): "I spent 33 years and four months in active military service and during that period I spent most of my time as a high class muscle man for Big Business, for Wall Street and the bankers. In short, I was a racketeer, a gangster for capitalism."

Part 4: The Rothschild Dynasty and the Architecture of War Finance
Historical Context
The Rothschild banking family established its influence in the late 18th and early 19th centuries. Mayer Amschel Rothschild built a banking operation in Frankfurt and dispatched his five sons to establish branches in London, Paris, Vienna, Naples, and Frankfurt — creating the first international banking network.

The Rothschild banks became the dominant issuers of government bonds in Europe during the 19th century. They financed both sides of the Napoleonic Wars. Nathan Rothschild's London operation famously used carrier pigeons and couriers to receive news of Wellington's victory at Waterloo before the broader market, enabling him to profit enormously from the information asymmetry — buying British bonds at depressed prices when the market falsely believed Napoleon had won, then selling at a massive profit when the truth emerged.

Critical Framing
It is essential to distinguish between analyzing the historical role of the Rothschild family in war finance and the antisemitic conspiracy theories that have been attached to their name for over two centuries. The Rothschild family was Jewish, and their success as bankers became the foundation for virulent antisemitic propaganda — particularly the fabricated "Protocols of the Elders of Zion," which fraudulently claimed to expose a Jewish conspiracy for world domination.

The Renaissance Protocol rejects antisemitic framing categorically. The issue is not the ethnicity or religion of any banking family. The issue is the structural incentive of private banking: when money creation is a private privilege, those who hold that privilege will use it to concentrate wealth and influence regardless of their identity. The Rothschilds were pioneers of this structure. They were not unique in exploiting it. Replace the Rothschild name with Morgan, Rockefeller, Warburg, or any modern financial institution — the structural pattern persists.

The Money Changers reference in the Renaissance Protocol draws from the biblical account of Jesus driving money changers and merchants from the Temple (Matthew 21:12-13). The complaint was not against any ethnicity. It was against the conversion of a sacred space into a site of profit extraction — specifically, the exchange of currency at exploitative rates and the sale of sacrificial animals at monopoly prices inside the Temple courts. The parallel is institutional, not ethnic: when the systems meant to serve human flourishing become instruments of extraction, the extraction must be confronted.

Part 5: Debt Slavery — The Modern Condition
The National Debt
As of 2024, the United States national debt exceeded $34 trillion. Annual interest payments on the debt have surpassed the defense budget. The government borrows money to service the interest on money it previously borrowed. This is the definition of a debt spiral.

Every dollar of national debt represents a claim by a creditor — banks, foreign governments, institutional investors — on future American labor. The taxpayer works to service interest on money that was created by banks and lent to the government. The principal was often created by the banking system. The interest is collected by the banking system. The labor that services the debt comes from the public.

The Personal Debt Trap
The same structure operates at the individual level:

Student loans. $1.7 trillion in outstanding student debt in the United States. Unlike most other forms of debt, student loans cannot be discharged in bankruptcy — a provision lobbied for by the lending industry. The average borrower pays hundreds of dollars monthly for years or decades on education that was supposed to increase their earning power.
Mortgage debt. A 30-year mortgage on a home typically results in the borrower paying approximately 2-3 times the purchase price in total when interest is included. The bank created the money for the loan from an accounting entry. The borrower pays it back with decades of labor.
Medical debt. The leading cause of personal bankruptcy in the United States. A system that charges extortionate prices for necessary care and then lends the money to pay for it at interest is not a healthcare system. It is an extraction system wearing the costume of medicine.
Credit card debt. Revolving consumer debt with average interest rates exceeding 20%. Compounding interest on consumption. The poorest borrowers pay the highest rates.
The Result
A population in debt cannot refuse to work. A population in debt cannot afford to protest. A population in debt cannot take risks, start businesses, invest in community, or resist exploitation. Debt is the mechanism by which nominal freedom is converted into functional servitude. The legal abolition of slavery did not end the extraction of labor by the financial system. It simply changed the instrument from chains to compound interest.

Part 6: The Alternative — Money as a Public Utility
Historical Precedents for Public Money
There are historical examples of money creation serving the public interest rather than private profit:

Colonial Script (Pennsylvania, 1723-1764). The Pennsylvania colony issued its own paper money, debt-free, to facilitate commerce. Benjamin Franklin testified before the British Parliament that this system was responsible for the colony's prosperity. The Currency Act of 1764, imposed by Parliament, prohibited the colonies from issuing their own money — forcing them to borrow from British banks. Franklin identified this as a primary cause of the American Revolution.
Lincoln's Greenbacks (1862). During the Civil War, President Abraham Lincoln authorized the issuance of United States Notes — debt-free money created by the Treasury — to finance the war without borrowing from banks at interest. The National Banking Act of 1863 subsequently created a national banking system that gradually re-privatized money creation. Lincoln reportedly stated: "The government should create, issue, and circulate all the currency and credit needed to satisfy the spending power of the government and the buying power of consumers." The attribution of this quote is disputed, but the policy of greenback issuance is documented fact.
The Chicago Plan (1933). Proposed by economists including Irving Fisher during the Great Depression, the Chicago Plan called for 100% reserve banking — eliminating the ability of commercial banks to create money through lending. All money creation would reside with a public authority. Banks would become intermediaries that lend existing deposits, not creators of new money. A 2012 IMF working paper by Jaromir Benes and Michael Kumhof revisited the Chicago Plan and concluded that it would "eliminate bank runs, dramatically reduce both public and private debt, and produce zero inflation" while allowing steady GDP growth. It was never implemented.
What the Renaissance Protocol Proposes
The Renaissance Protocol does not propose a new fiat currency. It proposes a value system rooted in measurable real-world contribution:

Proof-of-Build replaces Proof-of-Stake. Value is created by building things that benefit communities — housing, energy systems, food production, knowledge. Not by lending money at interest.
Build Credits are earned, not lent. They enter circulation through verified labor, not through bank loans. There is no interest. There is no debt creation. The money supply expands when communities build and contracts when they don't — directly tied to real productive activity.
The Community Treasury replaces the central bank. 10% of every mining reward flows to a DAO-governed Treasury. The Treasury funds community infrastructure. Decisions are made by builders, not bankers.
The buyout mechanism reverses privatization. The DAO purchases land and buildings and transfers them to an irrevocable Community Trust. Property is removed from the speculative market permanently. Housing becomes a right, not a rent-extraction vehicle.
The principle is simple: money should serve the people who create real value, not the institutions that create money from nothing.

Part 7: The Pattern and the Protocol
What This Case Demonstrates
Case Study 006 illustrates the Beast Framework applied to the monetary system:

Centralized Definition of Truth: The Federal Reserve and banking establishment define the nature of money — what it is, who creates it, and who benefits. Alternative systems (colonial script, greenbacks, the Chicago Plan) are excluded from mainstream economic discourse.

Incentive Alignment: Economists who support the current system receive tenure, publication, and influence. Economists who challenge it (Minsky, Keen, Hudson) are marginalized. Mainstream economics treats money creation as a neutral technical process rather than a political power distribution.

Suppression of Alternatives: Public money proposals are dismissed as inflationary or unrealistic, despite historical evidence and modern simulation (the 2012 IMF paper) suggesting otherwise. The Federal Reserve's structure, once understood, reveals that what is called "public" monetary policy is deeply entangled with private banking profit.

Digital Replication: Cryptocurrency, originally conceived as an alternative to central banking, has largely been captured by the same speculative dynamics it sought to replace. Proof-of-Stake systems replicate the dynamics of capital concentration. Trading platforms extract fees. The underlying architecture of money-as-debt remains unchallenged.
